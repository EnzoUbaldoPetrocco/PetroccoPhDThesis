\section{Introduction}
\label{ch1:sec:introduction}

The conceptualization of ``culture'' remains inherently complex \citep{Geertz73, Hofstede80, Leininger1988, Schwartz19921, Henare2007, Carrithers2010}, and no overarching consensus exists regarding its precise definition \citep{Hofstede2003}. 
A concise yet robust definition characterizes culture as a group's shared representation of the world, a perspective that has significantly influenced the ontological turn in anthropology over the past two decades \citep{Henare2007}. 

Historically, differing conceptualizations of culture have given rise to broad cultural taxonomies, most notably the distinction between Western and Eastern societies, as well as subsequent classifications based on national boundaries \citep{hofstede2001culture}. 
Although the East–West dichotomy has been widely criticized as overly reductionist, the conceptualization of culture at the national level continues to serve as a pragmatic heuristic \citep{fedriga2024representation} for preliminary cross-cultural analyses and as a useful first approximation. 
Moreover, treating culture as a national category facilitates statistical modeling, since empirical data are often collected and aggregated at the nation-state level, making national affiliation a tractable unit of analysis. 
However, some national cultures are disproportionately represented within the global technological landscape, whereas others remain systematically underrepresented \citep{not_uniform_data}. 
In addition, a nation-centric perspective inevitably obscures substantial cultural heterogeneity, particularly the distinct values, identities, and practices of sub-national communities and minority groups residing within the same geographical boundaries \citep{mcsweeney2002hofstede, venaik2013critical}.

These limitations pose a significant risk that minority cultures may be overlooked during the development of emerging technologies~\citep{not_uniform_data}, including social robots (SRs). 
Human–robot interactions (HRI) occur through both physical \citep{DESANTIS2008253, sharifi2021impedance} and verbal modalities \citep{verbalHRI} and span a variety of high-impact application domains, including healthcare \citep{HealthcareRobotics, ayari2020hybrid}, tourism \citep{tourismRobotics}, and education \citep{teachingRobotics}.
SRs are increasingly expected to operate in complex environments and engage in direct social interactions with humans \citep{sarrica2020many, henschel2021makes, AutonomousRobots, UserEngagementRobotics, Ray2008, 9143175, mccoll2016classifying}. 
Consequently, recent robotics research has focused on endowing these systems with cultural competence, motivated by the growing body of evidence demonstrating that culture profoundly influences both human perceptions of robots \citep{RobotCommunicating, Er04, Evers2008, Dingjun2010, Eresha2013, Joosse2014, Trovato2015, Mavrou2020, Andrist2015} and the broader quality of system design \citep{Jordan2022, Mohammed2015, PavingTheWayCulturallyCompetent}. 
According to \citep{Betancourt2003}, cultural competence in humans refers to the ability to effectively understand and act within different cultural contexts by appropriately interpreting norms, values, and behaviors. 
In social robotics, SRs are considered culturally competent systems when they can dynamically adapt their visual representations, perceptual processing, planning mechanisms, actions, and communicative behaviors to align with the user's worldview \citep{Papadopoulos2006, KnowledgeRepresentation}.
Developing culturally competent capabilities is therefore essential to ensure that robotic behaviors and their underlying AI-driven functionalities align with the diverse sociocultural expectations, norms, and preferences of human users \citep{Ray2008, Oneto2022}.

The challenge of cultural competence in SRs has been documented in the literature \citep{Bartneck2007, Rehm2013, Planning, GreetingSelection, Grassi_2022, CloudCultureDialogue, KnowledgeRepresentation, PavingTheWayCulturallyCompetent, Papadopoulos_CARESSES, papadopoulos2020caresses, 8673086}. 
However, early approaches relied on symbolic AI~\citep{PavingTheWayCulturallyCompetent, Bartneck2007, Rehm2013, Planning, Grassi_2022, CloudCultureDialogue, KnowledgeRepresentation} or neuro-fuzzy~\citep{bruno2016fuzzy} methods to address planning and decision-making problems. 
Such rule-based models are ill-suited for high-dimensional tasks, including natural language processing and computer vision, where fully specified formal models are generally unavailable.

ML models have been extensively employed for these tasks, achieving remarkable results \citep{DLRobotics, RLRobotics, MPRobotics, xu2020learning, bredeche2006perceptual, nguyen2020deep, zhou2022computer, arzani2020switching, hong2020multimodal, gao2021hybrid, yu2021deep}. 
However, within ML, cultural competence remains underexplored, particularly in scenarios where some cultures are underrepresented, which directly propagates into SRs that rely on such culturally biased models.

Scholars have identified two of the most critical issues in this context: the lack of architectural approaches addressing the culture-as-a-nation approximation, and the exclusion of minority cultures in statistical learning models such as ML systems.

This thesis is composed of two parts: the first focuses on the problem of underrepresentation; the second one focuses on proposing an alternative framework for solving culture-as-nation approximation. 
The research question addressed in the first part is:
\textit{How can the adverse effects of data scarcity, stemming from the underrepresentation of specific cultural groups, be mitigated to enhance the cultural competence of SRs?}
The research question addressed in the second part is:
\textit{How does a robot’s cultural competence influence user perception during navigational human--robot interaction in a residential scenario?}

The first part of this thesis, acknowledging the limitations of the culture-as-a-nation approximation, address the measurement and mitigation of cultural incompetence in ML models under underrepresentation scenarios. 
These scenarios rely on culture-as-a-nation approximations, where certain cultures are underrepresented relative to a dominant majority culture. 
This contribution consists of two categories of mitigation strategies: task-independent approaches, which modify the model architecture, and task-specific approaches, which modify the data.
After establishing the individual effectiveness of these techniques, further empirical evidence is provided showing that their strategic combination significantly improves the cultural competence of ML systems.

The experiments presented in the first section of this thesis are available in a GitHub repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/lucaoneto/CulturalCompetentML.git}\label{ch1:url:repodata}}, together with the two datasets collected.

The primary contribution of the second part is the development of an adaptive framework designed to address sub-national cultural heterogeneity in SRs. 
The proposed framework implements a probabilistic belief calibration mechanism based on culture-as-a-nation proxies. 
The framework begins from an initial state in which the user's culture is inferred using behavioral priors, and culture is implicitly represented within the knowledge of the Large Language Model (LLM) used for user interaction. 
The user subsequently confirms or refines these behavioral priors, with the LLM acting as a dynamic translation layer that converts explicit feedback into actionable behavioral and navigational parameters.

The experiments contained in the last part of this thesis are available in a GitHub repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/EnzoUbaldoPetrocco/CulturalCompetenceDuringNavigation}\label{ch1:url:ccn}}.
