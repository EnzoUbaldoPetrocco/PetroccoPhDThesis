\section{Discussion}
\label{ch3:sec:disc}

In the first part of this thesis, we explored two binary image classification use cases. Through dataset analysis, we observed that these problems remain challenging even when using a ResNetV2-50 backbone, as the inter-class distance is often smaller than the cultural distance between population groups. This imbalance can result in culturally biased performance, reducing cultural competence in scenarios where certain cultures are underrepresented in the dataset.

We validated this observation using three widely adopted classification approaches: Linear SVM, Gaussian SVM, and ResNetV2-50. Additional contributions of this first part include the introduction of the CIC metric and a mitigation strategy based on MTL, both of which proved effective in reducing culturally induced performance disparities.

Then I explored a complementary set of techniques based on DA. These approaches are further categorized into OS, ADV, and DM methods. The proposed techniques proved effective in mitigating cultural incompetence, and their combination with MTL yielded particularly strong results. Among the evaluated configurations, the MTL+PARB+DM combination achieved the best overall performance.

The second part of the thesis introduces a framework for designing culturally competent SRs. The proposed architecture is grounded in two premises enabled by recent advances in LLMs. An implementation of the framework was developed on the Pepper SR platform and evaluated through an ablation study assessing the contribution of each component to perceived cultural competence.

The results indicate that integrating cultural information can improve specific aspects of interaction; however, not all implementation levels can be considered culturally competent. Rather than producing uniformly positive outcomes, the different configurations exhibit clear trade-offs.

In particular, the culturally neutral configuration is generally perceived as more competent and reliable, whereas the culture-specific configuration is perceived as more socially proximate and relatable. Several factors may contribute to this outcome. Current models still exhibit forms of cultural incompetence, including hallucinations and uneven performance across languages. Furthermore, TTS and STT systems are often optimized for English and may perform less effectively in other languages, thereby affecting the overall interaction experience.

Real-time adaptation remains a significant challenge for LLM-based systems. Dynamic updates of linguistic and behavioral parameters introduce additional latency and may reduce interaction consistency. These limitations are further exacerbated in quantized models, which generally exhibit lower performance than their full-precision counterparts. Consequently, while LLMs enable new forms of cultural adaptation that were previously difficult to implement, achieving robust and reliable real-time cultural competence remains an open research challenge.

\section{Limitations and Future Directions}

The primary limitation of this work is that, although it develops two key components required for a culturally competent robot, it does not provide an integrated framework that combines them into a single system. Specifically, the dissertation addresses two fundamental aspects of cultural competence separately: culturally competent perception and culturally competent action and interaction. Given the novelty of this research area, we believe that investigating these components independently is a necessary first step toward understanding their individual challenges and requirements. The integration of these components into a unified architecture represents an important direction for future work.

The limitations specific to each of these two research directions are discussed separately in this section.

In the first parts of this dissertation,  the techniques presented for mitigating cultural incompetence in ML models have limitations in terms of applicability and computational cost. RT and diffusion-model-based approaches are highly task-dependent; while partial generalization to other domains is possible, their implementation remains non-trivial.

Furthermore, diffusion-model-based and ADV techniques require substantial computational resources and training time, which may not be justified in low CIC scenarios, where simpler approaches such as MTL or OS may be sufficient.

The MTL strategy can also become memory-intensive in scenarios involving multiple cultures, resulting in a large number of heads. In such cases, a phased training strategy may be adopted, in which a single head (e.g., corresponding to the majority culture) is trained first, followed sequentially by additional heads. This approach reduces peak memory usage by shifting part of the burden toward increased computational cost.

The experiments conducted in the first phase of this research are limited in both domain and task scope. While the current implementation focuses on specific vision tasks, ML models can address a broader range of culture-sensitive problems in the visual domain, including object detection and semantic segmentation. For such applications, MTL and DA remain valid and effective strategies to explore in future work.

When extending this framework to other modalities, natural language emerges as a primary vector for cultural transmission. This transmission occurs both explicitly (through vocabulary, dialect, and communicative style) and implicitly (through embedded norms and conventions). For example, a system recommending a dinner reservation at 20:00 may be perceived as unusually late in some regions but typical in others, illustrating latent cultural variability in linguistic interaction.

Further extensions of MTL architectures and CIC metrics may relax the hard constraint of national categorization, instead adopting distributional or density-based representations. While more computationally complex, such approaches may better capture fine-grained and underrepresented cultural variation within broader groupings.

First, a systematic comparative evaluation across a broader range of LLMs, particularly with respect to adherence to structured prompt constraints and cultural adaptation capabilities, would strengthen the empirical validity of the proposed architectural framework. Given the rapid pace of progress in LLM development, the use of a compressed and quantized model in this study may have introduced additional forms of cultural incompetence, consistent with the phenomena investigated in the first part of this dissertation. Future work should therefore explore whether the mitigation strategies proposed for reducing cultural incompetence in image classification systems can be extended to LLM-based architectures and evaluate their effectiveness in culturally adaptive HRI scenarios.

In addition, although the B and F paradigms do not implement runtime adaptation, occasional violations of the dialogue rules by the LLM resulted in follow-up questions regarding preferences in language, cultural background, or preferred interpersonal distance. These unintended behaviors may have reduced the perceived distinction between conditions and negatively affected evaluations of the A paradigm.

Second, systematically varying contextual factors, including spatial environments, robotic platforms, target cultural groups, languages, and interaction domains, would improve the generalizability of the findings. Such studies would help determine whether the observed trade-offs between cultural proximity and perceived competence are specific to the present experimental setup or represent more general properties of culturally adaptive robotic systems.

The results also highlight persistent performance disparities across cultural contexts. Consistent with observations from the vision-based experiments, these disparities are primarily attributable to imbalances in training data distributions. The relative underrepresentation of Italian and German linguistic and cultural corpora, compared to English-language data, contributes to reduced fluency and perceived competence in non-English interactions.

\section{Conclusion}

This dissertation investigated the impact of cultural underrepresentation in ML systems deployed in social robotics, proposing and evaluating multi-level strategies for mitigating these biases. The first parts of this thesis focused on algorithmic interventions in vision pipelines, demonstrating that cultural bias must be addressed through task-specific learning frameworks. Without such targeted interventions, systematic representational distortions persist, leading to operational and ethical limitations in HRI.

The second phase introduced an exploratory LLM-based cognitive architecture designed to model cultural frameworks through structured reasoning and runtime adaptation. This phase also revealed structural limitations in current generative models, which struggle to represent nuanced cultural variation without resorting to simplified or stereotypical representations. These limitations are further exacerbated by underlying data imbalances, which lead to instability in cross-cultural interaction settings.

Crucially, empirical evaluation of the adaptation strategy revealed a fundamental constraint in human–robot cross-cultural systems. While online adaptation mechanisms and LLM-driven cultural modeling can improve perceived alignment, their effectiveness is bounded by baseline system competence. When low-level execution or language understanding fails, high-level cultural adaptation does not yield measurable improvements in user experience and may even increase frustration. This establishes a key design principle: robust operational performance is a prerequisite for meaningful cultural adaptation.

This work contributes a structured framework for operationalizing cultural competence at the intersection of ML and social robotics. However, significant open challenges remain. CIC requires further formalization, particularly in terms of cross-domain applicability and measurement consistency. More broadly, larger-scale human-subject studies are required to validate and extend these findings.

It is also evident that culture remains insufficiently defined as an operational construct, which limits reproducibility and comparability across studies. Distributional approaches—modeling culture as a continuous statistical space rather than a categorical variable—represent a promising direction for future work, despite practical complications. While the proposed CIC metric can already be extended in this direction, fully non-categorical cultural representations remain an open research problem.

Finally, this research highlights persistent bias and performance degradation in quantized LLMs deployed in real-world systems. Despite their increasing use in robotic applications, these models often lose subtle alignment signals during compression, leading to degraded cultural sensitivity even in well-represented settings.

Ultimately, future research should prioritize empirical, human-in-the-loop evaluation to ensure safe and reliable deployment of culturally aware robotic systems. The results of this dissertation demonstrate that end-user studies provide essential diagnostic insights into system failures that are not observable through simulation alone. Cultural competence in social robotics must therefore evolve toward a framework that prioritizes not only successful interaction outcomes but also systematic analysis of failure modes and their underlying causes.
