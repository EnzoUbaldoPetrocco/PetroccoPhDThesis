This chapter introduces a suite of task-specific DA techniques designed to mitigate cultural bias in robotic visual perception systems. While the term DA traditionally refers to a broad range of methods used to increase dataset diversity and effective sample size, the approaches proposed in this chapter are specifically tailored to particular computer vision tasks.

Due to its conceptual simplicity and demonstrated effectiveness, DA has been widely explored in the computer vision literature. Although the underlying principles presented in this chapter may be extended to other modalities, their direct transferability is not guaranteed, as different data types exhibit distinct structural and statistical characteristics.

This chapter presents the proposed methodology and its empirical evaluation. 
The subsequent sections analyze the results obtained using the DA techniques and their combination with MTL mitigation strategy, resulted successful in the previous chapter. 
The chapter concludes with a discussion of the findings and their implications.


\section{Methods}
This section provides a description for the set of DA techniques that will be tested.
The four DA proposals can be clustered in four groups: oversampling (OS), random transformation (RT), adversarial sampling (ADV) and diffusion-based (DM) techniques.
The description of these mitigation strategies follows below in this order.

\subsection{Oversampling Techniques}
\label{ch2:sec:os}
The most fundamental DA strategy utilized is OS. 
In this approach, instances from under-represented cultural groups are replicated within the training distribution until an empirical balance across all cultural contexts is achieved.
A more sophisticated iteration of this technique, specifically designed for deep learning architectures, involves enforcing a Parity-Balanced Batch (PARB) strategy. 
Rather than static oversampling of the global dataset, PARB ensures that every stochastic gradient descent (SGD) iteration utilizes a mini-batch in which each cultural context $\mathcal{C}$ is represented with equal frequency.

\subsection{Random Transformations}

\label{ch2:sec:RT}
In various high-stakes applications~\citep{shorten2019survey}, particularly those involving high-dimensional input spaces such as computer vision, the empirical dataset $\mathcal{D}_n$ often lacks the cardinality required to fully encapsulate the underlying geometric and semantic invariances of the data-generating distribution $\mu$. 
To mitigate this limitation and suppress overfitting—thereby enhancing the generalization capabilities delineated in Section~\ref{ch2:sec:prel}—we utilize Random Transformations (RT).
Formally, we define a transformation mapping $T_\omega: \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathcal{X}$ parameterized by a random variable $\omega \in \Omega$. 
Here, $\Omega$ denotes the space of admissible transformations, which may include operations such as stochastic rotations, affine translations, or the injection of Gaussian noise. 
These transformations are constructed to be label-preserving, such that for any sample $(x_i, y_i) \in \mathcal{D}_n$, the augmented pair $(T_\omega(x_i), y_i)$ is assumed to represent a structurally valid realization from the support of $\mu$.

Within the scope of this research, we implement RT via two distinct operational strategies: Offline Random Transformation (OffRT) and Online Random Transformation (OnRT).
The OffRT approach involves the expansion of the training corpus $\mathcal{L}^r$ a priori, before the commencement of the training phase. 
We construct an augmented training set $\mathcal{L}^r_{\text{aug}} = \mathcal{L}^r \cup \{ (T_{\omega_i}(x_i), y_i) \}_{i=1}^{n_{\text{aug}}}$, where $n_{\text{aug}}$ denotes the cardinality of the synthesized samples. 
This effectively increases the sample size $n$ in Eq.~\eqref{ch2:eq:generalproblem}, facilitating the acquisition of a denser and more comprehensive representation of the input manifold.
Conversely, the OnRT strategy integrates the transformations dynamically into the model’s pipeline during the training phase. 
In this configuration, for every training epoch and each input instance $x$, a unique realization of $\omega$ is sampled, and the model processes the stochastically transformed input $T_\omega(x)$. 
This mechanism functions as a form of stochastic regularization—analogous to the dropout technique—thereby preventing the learning algorithm $\mathscr{A}_\mathcal{H}$ from memorizing specific training exemplars and instead incentivizing the extraction of invariant features.

Specifically, for the empirical evaluations conducted in this research, the space of admissible transformations $\Omega$ comprises horizontal reflections, stochastic rotations (within a bounded range of $g$ radians), the injection of additive Gaussian noise (with a standard deviation $\sigma = g$), and random rescaling (zooming). 
Within this framework, the variable $g$ serves as a scalar parameter governing the augmentation intensity of the RT methodology.

\subsection{Adversarial Sampling}
\label{ch2:sec:adv_sampling}

To further bolster the cultural competency of the classification framework, we incorporate an ADV strategy. 
The fundamental intuition underlying this approach is to augment the training distribution with synthesized samples designed to compel the model to decouple cultural identifiers from the primary discriminative task.

This objective is operationalized through a specialized pre-training phase utilizing the Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) algorithm \citep{madry2017towards}. 
Within this paradigm, adversarial perturbations are iteratively generated to maximize the model's loss, effectively identifying the "weakest" points in the decision boundary across different cultural strata. By training on these challenging variants, the model is incentivized to ignore superficial cultural correlations and instead converge upon more robust, invariant features.

Initially, we train a cultural discriminator $D_{\Theta_D}: \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}|}$ designed to predict the cultural provenance of a given input. 
We implement two distinct variants of this architectural strategy: the instantiation of a single, monolithic global discriminator (NODIV) or, alternatively, the deployment of class-conditional discriminators (DIV).The discriminator's parameters are optimized by minimizing the categorical cross-entropy loss function:\begin{align}\mathcal{L}D(x, y{c}) = - \sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{C}|} y_{c,i} \log(D_{\Theta_D}(x)i),
\end{align}
where $y{c}$ represents the ground-truth cultural label.

Upon completion of the training phase for $D_{\Theta_D}$, we synthesize adversarial perturbations $\delta$ to construct augmented inputs $\tilde{x} = x + \delta$. 
This process utilizes the PGD framework, which iteratively updates the perturbation to maximize the cultural classification loss while constraining the transformation within a predefined $\epsilon$-ball of the original image. For an iteration $t$, the update rule is defined as follows:
%
\begin{align}
x_{t+1} = \Pi_{x+\mathcal{S}} \left( x_t + \alpha \cdot \text{sign} \left( \nabla_{x_t} \mathcal{L}D(D{\Theta_D}(x_t), y_{c}) \right) \right),
\end{align}
%
where $\alpha$ denotes the step size and $\Pi_{x+\mathcal{S}}$ represents the projection operator onto the feasible set $\mathcal{S}$. 
This set is governed by the $L_\infty$ norm constraint $\| \tilde{x} - x \|_\infty \leq \epsilon$ and the requisite pixel intensity bounds $[0, 1]$.

The final classification hypothesis $f_{\Theta}$ is optimized over an augmented dataset $\tilde{\mathcal{D}} = \mathcal{D}_n \cup \mathcal{D}_{adv}$, where the adversarial subset $\mathcal{D}_{adv}$ comprises the synthesized inputs $\tilde{x}$ paired with their corresponding ground-truth labels $y$. 
By training on instances where cultural markers have been stochastically shifted or obscured through adversarial perturbations, the model is incentivized to ignore culturally dependent artifacts. Consequently, the learning algorithm converges upon more robust, invariant features necessary for the primary discriminative task, thereby mitigating cultural bias and enhancing cross-cultural generalization.

\subsection{Diffusion Models}
\label{ch2:sec:diff_model}

The tertiary DA strategy involves enriching the training corpus with synthetic imagery synthesized via a DM. Within the paradigm of generative modeling, the objective transitions from learning a discriminative mapping $f_\Theta: \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathcal{Y}$ to approximating the high-dimensional probability density function $\mu$ characterizing the input space $\mathcal{X}$. 
Diffusion models operationalize this by learning to invert a forward stochastic process that incrementally degrades the data through the sequential addition of Gaussian noise.

Consistent with the implementation provided in the framework, we define a diffusion schedule governed by a continuous temporal variable $t \in [0, 1]$. 
The transition from a noise-free image $x_0$ to its perturbed realization $x_t$ is characterized by the following linear interpolation:
%
\begin{equation}
x_t = \cos(\phi_t) x_0 + \sin(\phi_t) \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}),
\end{equation}
%
where the angular parameter $\phi_t$ is determined by a schedule that interpolates between a maximum signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) at $\cos(\phi_{\min}) = 0.95$ and a minimum SNR at $\cos(\phi_{\max}) = 0.02$. 
This specific trigonometric formulation ensures that the marginal variance of the latent variable $x_t$ remains invariant throughout the diffusion process, satisfying the constraint $\cos^2(\phi_t) + \sin^2(\phi_t) = 1$.

The model $f_\Theta$ is optimized to function as a denoiser. 
Specifically, the network architecture processes the perturbed image $x_t$ alongside the noise variance $\sin^2(\phi_t)$—which serves as a temporal conditioning signal—to predict the original noise component $\epsilon$. 
Consistent with the empirical risk minimization framework established in Problem~\eqref{ch2:eq:generalproblem}, the training objective employs the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) as the loss function $\ell$:
%
\begin{equation}
\min_\Theta \quad \hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{D}n, \epsilon, t} \left| \epsilon - f\Theta(x_t, \sin^2\phi_t) \right|_1.
\end{equation}
%

To address our specific objective, the diffusion methodology may be operationalized by leveraging the complete training distribution $\mathcal{L}$ for a given class, or by restricting the generative process exclusively to instances associated with a minority cultural context (DMMIN).

\subsection{Combining MTL and DA}
\label{ch2:sec:mtl_and_da}
The mitigation strategies delineated above may be synergistically integrated to address specific operational requirements. 
Within the scope of this thesis, and primarily due to the inherent sparsity of the empirical dataset, diffusion-based augmentation methodologies are consistently coupled with RT. 
Specifically, the training corpus is first expanded via OffRT; this augmented distribution subsequently serves as the input for the diffusion process, further amplifying the dimensionality and density of the dataset.
Provided that the transformation $T_\omega$ remains invariant with respect to cultural identifiers, the integration of RT and MTL is a mathematically straightforward procedure. 
Conversely, the concatenation of synthetic instances generated via DM or DMMIN within an MTL framework presents greater complexity, as it requires the assignment of cultural labels to synthesized data. 
In this research, we adopt a heuristic wherein synthesized samples are stochastically assigned to a minority cultural context.

Individual mitigation techniques may also be concatenated sequentially to amplify the overall debiasing effect.

\section{Mitigation Strategies}

Adhering to the methodologies delineated in the preceding section, we expanded the LAMP and CARPET datasets through a hierarchical application of OS, RT, and synthetic imagery generated via ADV and DMs.
This section describes the effect of this expansion on cultural competence.

The initial phase of this experimental series is dedicated to evaluating the baseline efficacy of fundamental strategies, specifically OS and RT. 
Subsequently, we provide a detailed exposition of the experiments involving adversarial sample generation, followed by an analysis of the performance gains derived from diffusion-based augmentation. Finally, we examine the synergistic effects of various hybrid configurations, integrating MTL with the aforementioned DA techniques to establish the most robust mitigation framework.

A primary distinction from the preceding RESNET configuration is the adoption of a ResNet50V2 backbone, succeeded by a structural sequence comprising a GlobalAveragePooling2D layer, a Dropout regularization layer, and a final Fully Connected (Dense) layer. 
This architectural transition is motivated by the documented performance enhancements associated with both the updated backbone architecture and the refined classification head design~\citep{szegedy2015going}.
Throughout these evaluations, the batch size was maintained at a constant $b=32$, and the fine-tuning learning rate was held at $l_{r_{fn}}=10^{-5}$. To differentiate this refined iteration from the initial RESNET implementation, it will be referred to as the Baseline in subsequent experimental discussions.

The initial DA strategies evaluated in this study comprise Oversampling (OS) and RT. Within our experimental framework, the RT pipeline consists of a sequential application of horizontal flipping, stochastic rotations, additive Gaussian noise, and random perturbations in brightness and zoom.
Specifically, the rotation and brightness factors were fixed at a magnitude of $0.01$. 
Conversely, the intensities of the Gaussian noise and spatial zoom were modulated by a unifying hyperparameter $g$. 
During the optimization phase, the learning rate $l_r$ was explored within the discrete set $\{10^{-4}, 5 \cdot 10^{-4}, \dots, 10^{-1}\}$, while the epoch count $n_e$ varied across $\{10, 15, \dots, 30\}$. The DA gain parameter $g$ was adjusted logarithmically across the interval $\{10^{-4.0}, 10^{-3.33}, \dots, 10^{0}\}$.

In accordance with the theoretical framework established in Section~\ref{ch2:sec:adv_sampling}, the adversarial sampling strategy is operationalized to bolster the cultural diversity of the training distribution through the synthesis of adversarial edge cases. 
This methodology centers upon the optimization of a cultural discriminator, $D_{\Theta_D}$, which serves as the fundamental reference for identifying and perturbing cultural boundaries.
The architecture of $D_{\Theta_D}$ is designed to mirror the convolutional backbone of the Baseline model, thereby ensuring feature consistency and a shared representation space across the experimental pipeline. 
To accommodate the multi-class nature of the cultural identification task, the classification head is transitioned from a binary to a categorical configuration, integrating a sequence of three dense layers culminating in a softmax activation function.

The PGD algorithm is implemented as an iterative optimization procedure, wherein the sign of the loss gradient derived from $D_{\Theta_D}$ is leveraged to perturb samples from a specific cultural class toward the decision manifolds of alternative cultures.The execution of this adversarial strategy necessitates the calibration of three primary hyperparameters: the maximum perturbation constraint $\epsilon$, the step size $\alpha$, and the iteration count $n_i$. 
These parameters were initially initialized based on established literature and subsequently refined through a qualitative assessment of the synthesized imagery; this ensures that the perturbed samples effectively traverse cultural boundaries while maintaining semantic and structural coherence.
The discriminator's training follows a bifurcated approach: the classification head is first optimized while maintaining a frozen backbone at a learning rate of $\eta = 10^{-3}$. 
This is followed by a comprehensive fine-tuning phase at $\eta_{\text{fine}} = 10^{-5}$, which ensures the generation of high-fidelity gradient information requisite for the subsequent adversarial attack.

The generative augmentation framework employs an Attention-Residual U-Net architecture, specifically optimized to approximate the score-matching function $f_\Theta(x_t, \sin^2\phi_t)$. 
The backbone of $f_\Theta$ is configured as a symmetric U-Net comprising four hierarchical stages with feature map dimensionalities of $[32, 64, 100, 128]$.
Within the encoder pathway, each stage incorporates dual residual blocks followed by average pooling and a dropout layer ($p=0.1$) to bolster model regularization. 
Temporal and noise-level context is integrated through 32-dimensional sinusoidal embeddings derived from the noise variance $\sin^2\phi_t$; these embeddings are concatenated with the feature maps to modulate the denoising trajectory. At the latent bottleneck, a Transformer module leveraging Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) is utilized to capture global spatial dependencies. The decoder pathway mirrors the encoder's topology, employing bilinear interpolation for spatial upsampling and skip connections to preserve high-frequency structural integrity throughout the reconstruction process.

The training procedure for the diffusion architecture is structured as a hierarchical paradigm to facilitate robust generalization. 
The model undergoes an initial pre-training phase on the \textit{Places} dataset \citep{zhou2017places}, specifically utilizing the \texttt{places365\_small} subset.
Given that training is conducted on an NVIDIA A100 GPU, the available VRAM was insufficient to process the entire dataset in a single pass. 
Consequently, the dataset was partitioned into $t_{pt}=15$ discrete segments, with each segment undergoing one epoch of training. To mitigate catastrophic forgetting during this sequential optimization, the learning rate is reduced by a decay factor of $d_{pt}=1.05$ upon the completion of each chunk. 
This iterative process is repeated for $n_{pt}=8$ cycles.
The optimization phase employs the AdamW optimizer with a weight decay of $10^{-4}$ and an initial learning rate of $l_r = 10^{-3}$, which is subjected to the aforementioned exponential decay at each iteration. 
This procedure yields a foundational diffusion model with established feature-generation capabilities.

Subsequently, a model selection phase is executed to align the denoiser with the target distribution $\mathcal{L}$. 
This involves a grid search over varying learning rates and the evaluation of a freezing strategy, wherein the initial $40\%$ of the network's layers are maintained as static parameters. 
The optimal configuration is identified by minimizing the Kernel Inception Distance (KID) on the validation manifold.
The final architecture is fine-tuned for $70$ epochs, incorporating an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) with a decay coefficient of $0.999$ to bolster the structural stability and fidelity of the synthesized imagery. 
During the inference stage, synthetic cultural instances are generated via a $12$-step reverse diffusion (sampling) process, transitioning from an isotropic Gaussian noise distribution to the target data manifold.

Furthermore, these experimental results confirm the presence of an under-representation bias; specifically, increasing the minority data threshold to $p_u = 20\%$ effectively mitigates these performance deficits, thereby validating the correlation between data availability and discriminator reliability.

\section{Conclusion}
%\color{red}Non presenta solo empirical evaluation ma introduce anche la metodologia. VERIFICA CHE TUTTE INTRODUZIONI E CONCLUSIONI SIANO COERENTI CON IL CONTENUTO!!!!
%\color{black}
This chapter presented the methodology and empirical evaluation of the proposed DA mitigation strategies for cultural underrepresentation bias across the LAMP and CARPET datasets. 
The experimental findings, spanning OS, RT, ADV, DM, and hybrid MTL configurations, consistently validate the effectiveness of the proposed framework.

The baseline augmentation strategies, OS and RT, demonstrated reliable improvements in minority cultural error rates and CIC across most configurations. 
Their combination proved particularly effective, suggesting that geometric variance and class-balancing operate synergistically rather than redundantly. The hybrid RT + OS configuration achieved the most substantial CIC reductions among the simpler strategies, with gains of up to $6.3$ percentage points on the LAMP dataset.

The adversarial sampling strategies, DIV and NODIV, further extended these improvements by enriching the training distribution with culturally boundary-crossing edge cases.
Analysis of the cultural discriminator revealed that minority underrepresentation directly degrades discriminator reliability, manifesting as measurable precision and recall deficits for minority classes. Despite this limitation, the mitigation strategy remained effective.
Both RT integration and a higher number of minority samples in the training set ($p_u = 20\%$) improved discriminator performance and enhanced the effectiveness of the mitigation strategy, confirming a correlation between discriminator quality and mitigation effectiveness. Furthermore, these results confirm that RT and ADV can be combined with positive effects.
Visual inspection revealed no perceptible differences between adversarially transformed samples across configurations, suggesting that the perturbations operate at a statistical rather than a visually salient level.

Diffusion-based augmentation, particularly DMMIN, produced consistent performance gains on both datasets, outperforming RT as a standalone strategy in most configurations. The minority-focused variant proved especially beneficial in scenarios of severe cultural scarcity, as its targeted training distribution more faithfully approximated the underrepresented cultural manifold. The LAMP dataset responded more readily to diffusion-based generation than the CARPET dataset, likely reflecting the greater visual complexity and intra-class variability inherent in carpet imagery.

The most substantial and consistent improvements were achieved through the hybrid MTL framework augmented with DA techniques. 
The combination of MTL with DM or DMMIN reduced both CIC and class-specific error rates across all datasets and majority culture permutations. The further integration of PARB at the head of the processing pipeline provided an additional regularization effect, yielding the strongest overall mitigation performance. Notably, the MTL + PARB + DM and MTL + PARB + DMMIN configurations achieved CIC reductions of up to $8.7$ percentage points on the LAMP dataset relative to the MTL baseline, underscoring the complementary nature of these strategies.

Taken together, these results establish a clear hierarchy of mitigation effectiveness: simple resampling and transformation strategies provide a reliable foundation, adversarial and generative methods add targeted distributional enrichment, and the full hybrid MTL + PARB + generative pipeline achieves the most robust reduction of cultural bias. The consistency of these findings across both datasets and all majority culture permutations supports the generalizability of the proposed framework.
