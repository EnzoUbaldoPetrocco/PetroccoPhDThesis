\section{System Architecture}
The proposed architectural framework is predicated on the premise that social robotic agents require the capacity to represent and reason upon environmental knowledge. 
To facilitate this, the system must acquire raw sensory data, abstract these inputs into semantic representations, perform high-level inference, and subsequently execute contextually appropriate behavioral responses via its actuators. 
With the recent advent of LLM-based agents, traditional system architectures can be dynamically restructured to integrate flexible reasoning components. 
This paradigm shift enables agents to autonomously adapt to their immediate deployment contexts while seamlessly incorporating distinct behavioral and cultural nuances.

Cultural competence may be integrated at various granularities and abstraction layers across each stage of this functional sequence. 
For instance, the semantic interpretation of sensory data—previously addressed in the context of inferring object states (e.g., a lamp) or detecting environmental features (e.g., floor coverings) from visual inputs—represents a critical intervention point. 
The proposed system architecture aims to encapsulate all such stages; consequently, the schema presented in Figure~\ref{ch5:fig:CCF} is intentionally abstract and generalized to ensure broad applicability across diverse robotic platforms and social contexts.

    \centering

    \caption{General Schema for Culturally Competent Social Robots.}
    \label{ch5:fig:CCF}
\end{figure}

Typically, cognitive architectures engineered for social robotics must integrate intrinsic social parameters. 
For example, consider the a priori linguistic assumptions inherent in human-to-human interaction: while an individual may default to the localized dominant language, they must possess the cognitive flexibility to transition to a common lingua franca upon encountering a non-native interlocutor. 
Within this architectural framework, language serves as a fundamental cultural variable that requires dynamic adaptation contingent upon the specific communicative requirements and preferences of the user.

While existing literature has extensively investigated the adaptation of robotic behaviors based on cultural parameters, these methodologies frequently exhibit restricted flexibility regarding the acquisition and manipulation of such knowledge. 
Furthermore, a significant portion of current architectures fails to account for intra-cultural heterogeneity—specifically "edge cases" wherein individual preferences diverge significantly from the statistical mode of a cultural demographic. 
For example, in various geographic regions, populations may prioritize regional dialects or minority languages over the official national language—a nuance often overlooked by models predicated on generalized or monolithic cultural datasets.

The proposed architecture exploits two fundamental characteristics of LLMs: first, having been pre-trained on expansive, heterogeneous datasets, these models can capture complex cultural distributions more effectively than classical, shallow ML approaches. This pre-training enables them to implicitly embed multi-layered cultural frameworks that shape the broader distribution of preferences within a national culture, including its constituent subcultures. Second, these generative architectures can be seamlessly integrated with external tools to execute targeted actions, effectively operating as a dedicated cultural adapter. Furthermore, the selection of LLMs is predicated on the premise that the most authoritative ground truth regarding user profiles is elicited directly from the participants themselves through natural language dialogue. This capability enables the system to interrogate the user in real time and dynamically refine latent cultural variables, ensuring a personalized and contextually aware interaction.

Within this architectural design, a specialized Cultural Adapter component is initialized utilizing Cultural Default Preferences as a priori information. This component leverages the core Cognitive Architecture as the primary user interface, while simultaneously maintaining the User Preferences component as a stateful repository for the runtime evaluation of the cultural variables. Through this systematic integration, the framework facilitates dynamic, online adaptation tailored to idiosyncratic human requirements.

However, the Cultural Adapter’s functional scope extends beyond knowledge acquisition; as a comprehensive framework, it must also facilitate the adaptation of sensory interpretation and communicative modalities. 
For instance, various Speech-To-Text (STT) engines demonstrate heterogeneous performance metrics across different linguistic domains. 
The adapter is therefore empowered to dynamically select the optimal STT engine predicated on linguistic data obtained during the initial heuristic phase or through subsequent real-time interactions. 
Correspondingly, the Text-To-Speech (TTS) engine is programmatically selected and configured based on the active language parameter to ensure phonetic and prosodic alignment with the user's cultural context.

The operational workflow is structured as follows: raw sensory inputs are ingested and processed by the LLM, which performs high-level optimization of parameters within a predefined set of cultural variables. 
Upon the establishment of these variables and their resultant values, the system executes a global reconfiguration of its constituent modules to ensure rigorous alignment with the inferred cultural context.

One might contend that the Cultural Adapter is redundant, under the premise that cultural domain knowledge is already inherently embedded within the latent representations of LLMs. 
However, conventional methodologies typically rely on injecting external knowledge through prompting techniques—such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)—or through fine-tuning, both of which lack the requisite flexibility for real-time, "on-the-fly" adaptation. 
In this regard, the proposed architecture may be conceptualized as a variant of the RAG paradigm; rather than merely retrieving static domain knowledge, the LLM is utilized to dynamically tune every constituent module of the robotic architecture.

\section{Measuring Culture and Cultural Competence}
\label{ch5:sec:measuring_cultural_competence}
Drawing from the preceding discussions, it is evident that a categorical, nation-centric approach lacks the requisite granularity to encapsulate the nuances of individual preferences and behaviors—elements that are indispensable for the advancement of culturally competent social robotics. 
To mitigate this limitation, we investigated methodologies for quantifying both the multi-dimensional cultural profiles of users and the subsequent cultural competence of robotic agents.

The granularity of these measurements dictates the formation of various cultural clusters. 
For instance, while demographic variables such as age, gender, and nationality serve as foundational targets for cultural measurement, higher-fidelity dimensions—such as specific aesthetic or musical preferences—offer deeper insights. 
Given the vast dimensionality of potential cultural features and the systemic scarcity of comprehensive datasets, an alternative metric for quantifying cultural alignment is required.

In the relevant literature, the Inclusion of Other in the Self (IOS) scale is frequently utilized as a psychological metric for the perceived distance between an individual and a conceptual entity. 
This can be operationalized through an Intersection Over Self (IOS) approach—a similarity measure between two sets defined as the cardinality of the intersection divided by the cardinality of the subject's set.

In this framework, the IOS facilitates the measurement of alignment between an individual's specific profile and broader constructs such as national culture or linguistic groups. 
This methodology enables a more granular understanding of the degree to which an individual’s preferences converge with or diverge from normative cultural expectations. 
Furthermore, this metric serves as a dual-purpose evaluative tool for robotic cultural competence: users can quantitatively assess the "intersection" between the robot's exhibited behaviors and their own internal cultural values.

While this metric effectively quantifies cultural proximity or "closeness," it is imperative to distinguish such proximity from the broader construct of cultural competence. 
To achieve a comprehensive evaluation of a robot's cultural aptitude, one must integrate the user’s perceived competence of the agent. 
The existing literature offers established psychometric instruments for this purpose, most notably the Robotic Social Attributes Scale (RoSAS), which is specifically designed to evaluate the multi-dimensional perceptions of SRs.

The following evaluative items are operationalized through the Inclusion of Other in the Self (IOS) scale. 
This pictorial measure assesses the perceived interpersonal or intercultural distance between the user (or their demographic identity) and the robotic agent, Pepper.

\begin{itemize}
\item Which illustration best characterizes the relationship between Pepper and your country of origin?
\item Which illustration best characterizes the relationship between Pepper and your national culture?
\item Which illustration best characterizes the relationship between Pepper and your individual preferences?
\end{itemize}

To evaluate the user's perception of the robotic agent, we utilized the Robotic Social Attributes Scale (RoSAS)~\citep{carpinella2017robotic}. 
Given that the experimental protocol required participants to complete this assessment across three distinct interaction strategies, we elected to reduce the number of measured dimensions to minimize the cognitive burden on the subjects.

\section{Cultural Parameters}
To operationalize the proposed paradigm, it is necessary to extract specific cultural parameters that can be encoded within the robot’s knowledge base and dynamically adapted via the LLM
\subsection{Language}
Language constitutes one of the most critical cultural parameters, as it not only reflects how individuals express themselves but also, according to certain linguistic hypotheses, influences cognitive processes. 
Consequently, it is reasonable to posit a reciprocal relationship wherein the primary language spoken informs communicative styles, and conversely, communicative needs shape linguistic choices.

Existing literature suggests that reliance on a lingua franca may not represent the optimal solution for effective intercultural communication~\citep{pavan2009communicating}. 
While individuals generally prefer to utilize their native tongue, the official national language does not always align with personal preferences within a specific geographic territory. 
The motivations for this divergence are multifaceted and highly contextual; in certain regions, local dialects or minority languages are so deeply entrenched that they are prioritized over the national standard. 
Furthermore, immigrant populations may exhibit a preference for their native language over the local vernacular.
\subsection{Communication Style}
Communication style pertains to the manner in which an individual conveys information, typically characterized by specific behavioral dimensions. 
However, a scientific consensus regarding these dimensions is lacking, and empirical validation through rigorous experimentation remains sparse in the existing literature.

One of the most prominent frameworks is the high-context and low-context continuum. Initially conceptualized by Hall~\citep{hall1973silent}, this dimension measures the degree to which message meaning is derived from the surrounding context versus explicit verbalization, resulting in varying levels of communicative directness. 
High-context cultures often prioritize group harmony and relational well-being over individual achievement, exhibiting collectivist traits. 
Conversely, low-context cultures favor explicit, direct, and task-oriented communication.

Empirically, Germans are categorized as a quintessential low-context culture~\citep{fenkl2024handbook}, whereas Italians are classified as high-context, particularly in comparative studies. 
For instance, Griggs~\citep{griggs1985going} ranked 15 countries, identifying Germany as having the lowest context and Italy as occupying a median position (7th). Beyond Hall's work, Hofstede utilized statistical analysis to define six foundational cultural dimensions~\citep{hofstede1984culture}, which have subsequently been mapped to communicative behaviors~\citep{smith2011communication,house2006communicative}. 
While Smith et al.~\citep{smith2011communication} acknowledge that subcultural behaviors can occasionally be orthogonal to national averages, they maintain that most subcultures remain largely coherent with national norms.

From a ML perspective, these findings suggest that initializing a system with national-level cultural priors—while remaining receptive to learning idiosyncratic individual preferences—constitutes an effective adaptive strategy, even when specific user parameters contradict national-level assumptions.

It is also pertinent to highlight the divergent attitudes toward "small talk" within German and Italian communicative norms; empirical evidence suggests that Germans exhibit a lower propensity for phatic communication than their Italian counterparts~\citep{house2006communicative}. 
This distinction fundamentally informs the execution of the country-based strategy, particularly in autonomous modes where the robot does not solicit direct user feedback.

Conversely, as Italians demonstrate a higher inclination toward small talk, the system should engage in brief, rapport-building dialogue during feedback intervals. 
In contrast, the culture-independent strategy should maintain a neutral communicative baseline, eschewing specific cultural sociability markers to serve as a control or a generalized interface.
\subsection{Proxemics}
Proxemics, as established in the literature, exerts a significant influence on the perception of agency and social presence. 
Extensive empirical research corroborates that proxemic behavior is mediated by cultural variables, a phenomenon observed not only in interpersonal dynamics but also within the domain of human-robot interaction (HRI). 
However, spatial preferences are not governed exclusively by cultural norms; they are influenced by a confluence of environmental and individual factors, which complicates the initialization of optimal spatial parameters within novel interactive contexts.

To ascertain appropriate comfort distance parameters within this specific framework, a preliminary online study was conducted to establish the ground-truth initial proxemic values. 
It should be emphasized that this empirical investigation is essential, as the criteria for optimal interpersonal distance between a robot and a human are highly contingent upon the situational context.

\section{Additional Cultural Dimensions}

Culture both influences and is influenced by trust in technology and individual personality traits. 
Consequently, it is pertinent to incorporate validated psychometric assessments to evaluate the participants' technological trust and personality profiles.
These metrics, integrated with the Inclusion of Other in the Self (IOS) scale for assessing cultural proximity, are essential for estimating the values of various cultural dimensions.

The authors acknowledge that numerous other factors contribute to the multifaceted construct of "culture"; however, the scope of this study is intentionally delimited. 
This limitation is methodologically justified by the requirement to mitigate the cognitive burden on participants and maintain the internal validity of the experimental results.

Trust in technology, and specifically within the domain of Social Robotics, constitutes an expansive field of research characterized by a plurality of evaluative frameworks.

Crucially, closed-ended quantitative metrics are not the sole mechanism for evaluating cultural competence or architectural efficacy during human-robot interactions. Open-ended qualitative inquiries, which allow participants to elaborate extensively on their subjective perceptions of the experience, can uncover critical phenomenological perspectives that might otherwise be overlooked. Furthermore, incorporating non-verbal behavioral analysis can introduce valuable nuances to the evaluation framework.

However, because experimental investigators may harbor implicit biases when evaluating their proprietary architectures against baseline solutions, a double-blind methodological approach is highly recommended. Specifically, employing independent annotators to evaluate and code the participants' behavioral responses separately—subsequently measuring inter-rater reliability to merge the datasets—constitutes a rigorous strategy for mitigating evaluator bias. For example, a structured coding scheme consisting of predefined behavioral descriptors can be provided to the annotators, enabling the quantification and frequentative analysis of specific behavioral occurrences.
