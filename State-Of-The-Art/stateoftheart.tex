The integration of cultural factors in robotic systems has been a subject of rigorous investigation over the past decade \citep{Bartneck2007, Rehm2013}. 
Predominantly, existing research has concentrated on identifying the determinants of system acceptability across diverse cultural cohorts, focusing on either morphological appearance or behavioral patterns \citep{Shibata2009, Dingjun2010, Lee2016, vernon2024african, papadopoulos2022transcultural, recchiuto2019designing, akinade2023culturally, ornelas2023redefining, zantou2023culturally, Papadopoulos_CARESSES, battistuzzi2021socially, papadopoulos2020caresses, 8673086}. 
This includes the study of cultural nuances in verbal and non-verbal communication \citep{Rau2009, Andrist2015, Bruno2018, Rehm2018, CloudCultureDialogue, bruno2016fuzzy, chugh2024aisles, obremski2022impact, nias2020culturebot, recchiuto2020feasibility, GreetingSelection, pena2019eeva}, as well as variations in perceived social distance and proxemics \citep{Eresha2013, Joosse2014, Trovato2015, truong2016towards}.

Beyond social cues, scholarship has addressed cultural competence within specific AI sub-domains, such as automated planning \citep{Planning, fedriga2023linguistic}, knowledge representation \citep{GreetingSelection, KnowledgeRepresentation, Grassi_2022, mansouri2020can, bringsjord2020culturally}, and activity recognition \citep{Chiang2019}. 
However, as evidenced by recent systematic and scoping reviews concerning SR in geriatric assistance, these prior approaches have generally failed to establish a unified conceptual framework. 
There remains a critical lack of generalized architectures designed to imbue intelligent systems for health and social care with comprehensive cultural competence \citep{Abdi2018, Pu2019, Maresova2020, Lee2020}. %Lee2020,  %Despite its seminal role 

Recently, a conceptual framework was established\footnote{https://cordis.europa.eu/article/id/124441-the-worlds-first-culturally-sensitive-robots-for-elderly-care} to imbue Socially Assistive Robots (SAR) for geriatric care with cultural competence \citep{PavingTheWayCulturallyCompetent, KnowledgeRepresentation, Papadopoulos_CARESSES}. 
This framework was developed by synthesizing foundational research from Transcultural Nursing \citep{Leininger1988} and established paradigms in culturally competent healthcare \citep{Betancourt2003}.

Anthropological studies of material culture demonstrate that everyday objects, and the domestic interiors in which they are situated, exhibit distinct design variations across different global regions—even when such objects share functional equivalence. 
This divergence is anticipated, as object design is intrinsically linked to the specific social tasks they serve and the regional availability of materials utilized in their fabrication \citep{Robb2015}. Consequently, while certain aesthetic universals have been identified \citep{Hardonk1999, Hekkert2008}, specific visual features tend to recur with greater frequency and coherence within particular geographical and cultural domains.

Metaphorically, two objects may possess identical functional affordances \citep{Gibson1986} yet be characterized by distinct "cultural signatures".
These features constitute critical data that ML algorithms must account for during the classification process to ensure robustness. 
However, existing computational solutions have largely overlooked these nuances, neglecting the essential role of cultural competence in ML optimization.

From a strictly computational perspective, contemporary research has explored various principles and architectures for the design of culture-aware ML models \citep{miehle2021multimodal, khan2021one, dmytryk2022mining, karpouzis2024plato}. 
Significant emphasis has been placed on the implementation and evaluation of NLP techniques tailored for cultural sensitivity \citep{blanchard2024cultural, bhatia2023gd, almaliki2016persuasive, dlHumorRecognition, albuquerque2024hype, Wu2012TheAO, cirucci2024culturally, kuo2024identification, dwivedi2024navigating}.

Additional research trajectories include the integration of cultural awareness into generative image tasks \citep{Quan2018, Zhou2022445, yun2024cic, mohamed2022artelingo} and classification problems across diverse domains. 
These range from analyzing vaccine hesitancy \citep{alharbi2023cultural, alharbi2024deep} and digital discourse regarding mental health during the COVID-19 pandemic \citep{castilla2022applying}, to AI-augmented clinical triage \citep{jordan2023impact} and personalized recommender systems \citep{yohe2023towards}.

Within this broader context, the first and second parts of this thesis examines the visual representation of objects and environments across diverse cultural contexts \citep{Knappett2005} and evaluates the subsequent impact on ML performance in classification tasks.
The last part of this thesis, integrates LLMs implicit knowledge and capacity to map user's intent into system's parameters shift.

ML has achieved unprecedented performance benchmarks, frequently exceeding human-level proficiency across diverse domains \citep{openai2023gpt4, alphago, minerva, human_performance}. 
These advancements have catalyzed researchers and practitioners across various disciplines to leverage ML in addressing domain-specific challenges. 
This paradigm shift has resulted in a significant proliferation of successful ML applications, establishing the technology as a ubiquitous force. 
Consequently, ML continues to revolutionize and profoundly influence the broader societal landscape \citep{MLForSociety, MLTrends}.

ML serves a pivotal role in robotics, significantly enhancing the capacity of autonomous agents to perceive and interact with complex environments \citep{DLRobotics, RLRobotics, MPRobotics, xu2020learning, bredeche2006perceptual, nguyen2020deep, zhou2022computer, arzani2020switching, hong2020multimodal, gao2021hybrid, yu2021deep}.
Within the SR domain, ML is equally critical; it provides the sophisticated computational frameworks necessary for robots to interpret human environments. 
These capabilities allow agents to leverage environmental and social data to optimize interactions with human users across both verbal and non-verbal modalities \citep{SocialRobotics, zheng2013designing, jacob2015optimal, saunderson2020investigating, ogenyi2019physical, rakovic2022gaze, alsamhi2022autonomous}.

The pinnacle of ML models are, LLMs\citep{raiaan2024review} and LLM-based autonomous agents\citep{luo2025large}. 
Driven by scalable transformer architectures and aligned via reinforcement learning from human feedback, LLMs have transcended traditional static text-to-text processing to manifest sophisticated in-context learning\citep{dong2024survey}, semantic comprehension, and zero-shot\citep{kojima2022large} generalization capabilities. 
Crucially, the integration of these models into agentic frameworks has enabled a transition from passive generative systems to proactive decision-making entities. 
By anchoring the core LLM within a modular architecture that incorporates explicit planning algorithms, short- and long-term memory structures, and external tool-calling execution loops.

Despite these achievements, the integration of ML into technologies designed for social environments and human interaction has been accompanied by escalating concerns regarding its ethical impact \citep{weidinger2021ethical, MLEthicsLaw, PhenotypesML}. 
These concerns stem from the prevailing design paradigm, which prioritizes high-level technical performance benchmarks—such as predictive accuracy and computational efficiency \citep{regressionMetrics, rlMetrics, rlMetrics2, semi-supervisedMetrics, multi-class-metrics, ComputationalMetrics}—often at the expense of human-centric metrics. 
Failure to incorporate these dimensions into the technological design process can precipitate significant ethical dilemmas \citep{electronics10050593, Human_metrics, ECThicGuidelines}.
Critical among these dimensions is sustainability, which involves minimizing energy consumption and promoting the reusability of models to mitigate environmental impact \citep{strubell_etal_2019_energy}. Transparency and explainability are equally vital, ensuring that decision-making processes are interpretable so that users can evaluate and establish trust in the system's outputs \citep{ExplainabilityRequirements, causabilityMedicine, guidotti2018survey, XMLMetrics}. 
Furthermore, fairness must be addressed by identifying and mitigating algorithmic bias to prevent discriminatory outcomes and ensure equitable decision-making across diverse demographics \citep{fairness, Onetofairness, AccuracyvsFairness}. 
Privacy and security requirements necessitate robust safeguards against unauthorized data access and adversarial exploitations to protect individual integrity \citep{liu2020machine, Biggio_2018, SecML}. Finally, cultural competence involves developing systems capable of dynamically adapting to the specific cultural environments and norms in which they operate \citep{survey_fairness_wreprbias}. 

Preliminary efforts have been made to integrate cultural competence into ML frameworks \citep{dlHumorRecognition, miehle2021multimodal, almaliki2016persuasive, khan2021one, dmytryk2022mining, karpouzis2024plato, Wu2012TheAO, dwivedi2024navigating, cirucci2024culturally, Quan2018, Zhou2022445, yun2024cic, mohamed2022artelingo, alharbi2023cultural, alharbi2024deep, jordan2023impact, castilla2022applying, yohe2023towards, blanchard2024cultural, bhatia2023gd, albuquerque2024hype, kuo2024identification}. 
However, this research trajectory remains in its nascent stages and is characterized by significant limitations, particularly concerning its application within the SR domain.

The first primary limitation is that existing scholarship in this domain focuses predominantly on data diversity \citep{not_uniform_data, seaborn2023not, lim2021social, hagerty2019global, stacy2025data, ijerph21121642, li2024health, lee2024negotiating, zhang2024ameliorating, blanchard2015socio, thakkar2024artificial}, empirically demonstrating that contemporary datasets either omit or under-represent a vast plurality of cultures \citep{ijerph21121642, li2024health, lee2024negotiating, zhang2024ameliorating, blanchard2015socio, thakkar2024artificial}.

A second critical gap is the absence of standardized benchmark datasets specifically curated for cultural diversity. 
Such benchmarks are essential for researchers to rigorously evaluate the cultural competence of ML models within the SR domain \citep{Facial_recogn_eval_datasets, BiasDataset, not_uniform_data, seaborn2023not, lim2021social, hagerty2019global, stacy2025data, ijerph21121642, li2024health, lee2024negotiating, zhang2024ameliorating, blanchard2015socio, thakkar2024artificial}, analogous to the established evaluation frameworks currently utilized in algorithmic fairness research \citep{le2022survey}.

The third limitation concerns the absence of robust mitigation strategies for cultural incompetence in ML for SR. 
There is a discernible lack of methodologies that can be effectively generalized and applied across broad, large-scale ML environments. 

It is critical to note that the aforementioned studies—addressing social navigation \citep{patompak2017learning}, user clustering \citep{santos2018extended}, and gesture generation \citep{Gestures}—predominantly operate under the implicit assumption of a uniform data distribution across different cultural groups. 
However, this assumption is frequently invalidated by empirical evidence. 
Subsequent research has highlighted significant disparities in data distribution, emphasizing that minority cultural contexts are often underrepresented or systematically excluded from foundational datasets \citep{not_uniform_data, seaborn2023not, lim2021social, hagerty2019global, stacy2025data, ijerph21121642, li2024health, lee2024negotiating, zhang2024ameliorating, blanchard2015socio, thakkar2024artificial}.

The rows of Table~\ref{ch1:tab:related} categorize the functional domains where cultural competence is critical: Design, Verbal Interaction, Non-verbal Interaction, Social Distance and Navigation, Planning, Knowledge Representation, Generative Tasks, Activity Recognition, and Object Classification. 
The columns distinguish the methodological focus of the cited works: those implemented in social robots without the use of ML (SR), generic ML techniques not specifically contextualized for social robotics (ML), and ML frameworks specifically engineered for social robot applications (SR+ML).

As indicated in the table, this thesis represents the primary effort to address object classification and SR design using ML architectures specifically designed for SRs and to experimentally evaluate the problem of embedding cultural competence using LLM-based architectures. 
The only other research identified within the first functional category pertains to a medical application, which underscores the performance disparities that arise when models encounter divergent cultural datasets. 
These findings further validate the necessity of developing robust mitigation algorithms to address cultural incompetence in ML models and social robotics.

Within the aforementioned literature, certain studies conceptualize cultural competence as a derivative of algorithmic fairness constraints \citep{karpouzis2024plato, albuquerque2024hype}. 
While a nexus between these two fields exists, their objectives are fundamentally divergent: whereas fairness constraints seek to decorrelate a model's output from sensitive variables, cultural competence aims to actively leverage cultural information as a feature to optimize model performance across all cultural cohorts. 
Notwithstanding these conceptual differences, such studies are included here to provide a comprehensive overview of the current state of the art.

To the best of our knowledge, no prior research has explicitly addressed culturally competent object classification within the social robotics domain or experimentally addressed the problem of culture as a nation approximation.
